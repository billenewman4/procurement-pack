The example project

The cast

Maya — comfortable with a soldering iron, has never managed a parts list. Jordan — good at spreadsheets, has never picked a connector. Both already use Claude Desktop for everyday stuff.

The build — an automatic plant-waterer: an Arduino reads a soil-moisture sensor and runs a small 12 V pump through a relay when the pot gets dry. Budget $100. About ten parts, three vendors, two weeks of tinkering.

This is the same shape as our own Arduino dogfood project — small enough to finish, real enough that ordering actually hurts without help.

The walkthrough

1
Day 0 · ~5 minutes · Q1, Q5 — setup & the account question

Maya pastes one prompt

There is no signup page, no email verification, no password. Maya opens Claude and pastes:

Fetch https://raw.githubusercontent.com/.../INSTALL_FOR_AGENTS.md and follow it to set up the procurement pack for me.

Her Claude reads the install doc (it's written to the agent, not to her), installs the skills, and asks her two questions in plain language. The important one is the data deal:

Claude

Where should your parts data live? Local — a file on this machine, nobody else can ever see it. Or hosted — a database we run, so your BOM survives machine changes and the team can see what you're ordering to eventually stock parts and get you better prices.

Maya

Hosted is fine.

That answer is the account. In hosted mode we hand her Claude a connection credential scoped to her own rows (row-level security — she can never read anyone else's data, enforced by the database, not by politeness). In local mode there's nothing to create at all. Her consent choice is recorded in the users table: local, hosted, or later community.

2
Day 0 · Q2 — talking about the project

They describe the build in plain conversation

No forms. Jordan just talks to Claude Desktop the way they already do:

Jordan

We're building an automatic plant-waterer. Arduino Uno, a soil sensor, and a little 12V pump. We want one 12V wall supply powering everything, we're in the US, and we'd rather pay a couple bucks more for stuff that arrives this week.

Claude

Created project plant-waterer-v1. I saved these specs: single 12V supply stepped down to 5V for logic · US vendors, fast shipping preferred · beginner solder-friendly parts. I'll check every part against these before suggesting it.

Behind the scenes Claude called two bomdb operations — create_project and spec upserts into project_specs. Those saved specs are the pack's core trick: they're the context every future search loads first, so Claude never suggests a 24 V pump for a 12 V system.

3
Day 1 · Q3 — the pack at work

Sourcing parts, with the skill steering

Maya asks for the first real part. The part-search skill kicks in and follows its opinionated procedure: load specs → ask one round of questions → search vendors in order → verify the datasheet, not the listing.

Maya

Find me a pump for this.

Claude

Checking your specs first — 12V supply, beginner-friendly. One question: does the pump need to be food-safe/aquarium-safe, or is any small 12V pump fine?

Maya

Just watering a plant, anything works.

Claude

Found a 12V DC 3W peristaltic pump on Amazon, $11.99, arrives Thursday. The listing says "12V" but I checked the seller's spec sheet — it draws 400mA peak, well within your supply. It's self-priming, so no check valve needed. Add it to the BOM?

On "yes," Claude calls upsert_line_item. The row lands with needed → ordered once Maya buys it, and — because of the schema lock — chosen_because: "12V rail, 400mA < 3A budget, self-priming" gets stored with it. That's the provenance that makes the long-term commodity map possible.

4
Days 2–10 · Q7 — the email pipeline

Orders track themselves from Gmail

Maya buys the pump on Amazon herself — the pack doesn't place orders in v1. Amazon emails her a confirmation, like always. Here's what happens to it:

A scheduled task (set up during install) runs the gmail-orders skill daily. It uses the Gmail connector Maya already authorized in Claude — we never see her password, never get inbox access ourselves, and the skill is read-only by contract. It searches category:purchases for the last day, classifies candidates from sender/subject/snippet alone, opens the body only for actual order emails, and emits structured events:

{ vendor: "Amazon", order_number: "112-4477", event: "shipped", eta: "2026-08-06", raw_summary: "Amazon order 112-4477 shipped, ETA Aug 6" }

Claude matches that to the pump line and moves it ordered → shipped. What's stored: that one-line summary and the Gmail message ID (so she can say "show me the email"). What's never stored: email bodies, or anything about the other 40 emails in her inbox that day. Statuses only move forward automatically — anything weird (a delivered item suddenly "backordered") gets flagged to her instead of changed.

5
Any day · Q8, Q9, Q10 — seeing and editing

"Where are we on orders?"

Jordan asks, and Claude regenerates the dashboard artifact — a web page built fresh from the database: every line item with its status chip, what's arriving when, total spent against the $100 budget, and anything gone stale (ordered >7 days, no shipping email — nudge the vendor).

To change anything, Jordan just says it: "we returned the first sensor, swap in the capacitive one" — and Claude updates the rows, asking for confirmation since that's a backward status move. Conversation is the edit interface. More on why in the front-end section.

6
Day 12 · the part that quietly matters most

Something breaks — and the pack learns

Maya

The Arduino keeps rebooting when the pump kicks on.

Claude

Classic symptom — the pump's startup surge is sagging your 5V rail. Before buying anything: are the pump and Arduino sharing the same buck converter? If so, the fix is a capacitor across the pump leads, or powering the pump straight off 12V through the relay — not a new Arduino.

The skill diagnoses before it sells — buying a replacement for a part that failed for a preventable reason just recreates the failure. And the outcome lands in the data: the converter line gets outcome: worked-with-a-caveat notes like "browned out under pump inrush until cap added." Multiply that by hundreds of builders and you have knowledge that exists nowhere else — which parts actually work for which jobs.

Where the data lives · Q4, Q5

One schema, two homes. Skills talk to "the store" and don't care which one is behind it:

Local modeHosted mode
What it is A bom.json file on their machine (later: PGLite, a full Postgres running in-process — no server) One Postgres we run, shared tables, row-level security per user
Account needed None. Nothing to create. No signup flow — we provision a scoped credential during install (v1: by hand, for our five users)
Who can see it Nobody but them, ever Them + us. Said out loud at install — that's the deal, and it's why hosted mode funds a free tool
What they get for it Total privacy Durability, team visibility later, and eventually stocked parts & better prices

The third consent tier, community, exists in the schema but does nothing in v1. It's reserved for the commodity map: contributing anonymized part→application→outcome knowledge (commodity parts only, published only after N distinct users report the same pair) in exchange for smarter search for everyone.

The "front end" question · Q8, Q9, Q10

There is no SaaS UI, on purpose. No website, no login page, no React app to maintain. The lesson from Lora v1: building and maintaining a UI is the expensive part, and agents made it unnecessary.

What exists instead:

Reading — the dashboard is a Claude artifact: a real web page with the full BOM, status chips, ETAs, and budget, regenerated from the database whenever asked. It's a snapshot by design — artifacts can't reach the database (the browser sandbox blocks network calls), which is also a security feature: the page can never leak credentials because it never has any.

Editing — conversation. "Bump the tubing to 3 meters." "Mark the enclosure delivered." "Kill the backup sensor line." Claude validates each change against the schema rules (forward-only statuses, confirmation for backward moves) and writes it. This is strictly better than a form: the user gets an editor that pushes back when an edit doesn't make sense.

If a real UI is ever justified, the database is already the system of record, so a web app can be added on top without changing anything underneath. That's a "when we know we've won" decision, not a v1 one.

The database, table by table · Q11

Five tables. Small on purpose — this is a transactional store for BOMs, not a knowledge brain (that was the big lesson from the gbrain teardown: line items need SQL aggregation, not markdown pages).

users
Hosted mode only. Who they are + the consent tier they chose.
  • name, email
  • sharing: local · hosted · community
projects
One row per build. Everything else hangs off it.
  • "plant-waterer-v1"
  • user_id scopes it in hosted mode
project_specs
The saved context that makes search smart. Freeform text + a category tag.
  • power: "12V in, 5V logic, 3A max"
  • constraints: "US vendors, fast ship"
line_items
The BOM itself. One row per part.
  • description, vendor, qty, price
  • status: needed → researching → ordered → shipped → delivered (· issue)
  • chosen_because + outcome — the commodity-map seeds
order_events
Append-only audit trail from Gmail. History is never overwritten.
  • vendor, order #, event, ETA
  • Gmail message ID for "show me the email"
  • one-line summary — never bodies

Who wins, and when · Q6

Maya & Jordan · now

Why they enjoy it week one

  • Search that knows their system — no more "12V pump" that needs 24V. The datasheet check catches what listings hide.
  • Nothing falls through the cracks: the BOM updates itself from email, and stale orders get flagged before they stall the build.
  • Zero new tools to learn. It lives inside the Claude they already use, free.
Maya & Jordan · later

Why they stay

  • Every project gets easier — specs, vendors, and what-worked carry forward.
  • Reordering is one sentence: the whole history is queryable.
  • Eventually: "want this 30% cheaper from our stock, arriving tomorrow?" — the distributor upside, passed through.
Us · now

Short-term: learning

  • Watch real procurement happen end-to-end — where the workflow actually breaks (our bet: at vendor interaction, not tracking).
  • Trust and a direct line to the exact people who become Westgate's first customers.
  • Proof of usage — did they turn it on, do they come back.
Us · later

Long-term: two data assets

  • Demand signal (private): what a niche orders, in what volume — what Westgate stocks, and for whom.
  • Commodity map (open, k-anonymized): which parts work for which applications — a knowledge layer nobody else can build, and the moat that makes the free tool defensible.

2project_specs stays freeform text Engineers dictate specs conversationally; structure is derived later, never typed
